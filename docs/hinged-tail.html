Hinged Tail Drive

The line shaft leaves the hull through its bottom into a skeg that carries the keel line aft at full depth. At the skeg's heel, just behind the transom, a short tail carries the propeller on its own shaft. The tail hangs from a gimbal. Its tilt pin and its steering pin both pass through the centre of a constant-velocity (CV) joint, so the tail can swing up out of the way, or side to side to steer, and the joint keeps driving the propeller.

If you know a Thai long-tail boat, this is the same idea scaled down to a pedal kayak, with only the tail moving.

How it behaves

Running

The tail rests on a stop 8° below horizontal, so 99% of the thrust pushes forward. Its own weight and a light spring hold it there. Thrust runs along the tail and straight through the joint, so it neither pushes the tail down nor lifts it.

Touching bottom

A fin under the tail reaches about 25 mm below the propeller tips, so it hits ground first. A blow from ahead lands below the hinge and swings the tail up and back, the way a kick-up rudder works.

Launching from the cart

A line from the end of the tail runs over a sheave on the transom to a cleat in the cockpit. Pull it to raise the propeller clear of the water. Then the boat draws only what the hull draws, about 150 mm. The skeg and the gimbal sit no lower than the keel, so they add nothing.

Reverse

Pedalling backwards reverses the propeller. Its thrust also runs through the joint, so the tail stays down without a latch.

Steering

The tail swings ±25° on a vertical pin and steers by aiming the thrust. It steers even at walking pace and in reverse, and it replaces the rudder. That leaves one appendage at the stern that has to kick up, not two.

Hull penetration

The line shaft passes through the hull bottom into a tube inside the skeg, with a lip seal at the skeg's heel about 125 mm below the waterline. The pressure on the seal is small.

How low the hinge goes

A lower hinge points the thrust closer to horizontal and runs the joint straighter. But the hinge can't lift, so anything below the keel adds to the draft for launching. The table shows each hinge height with the tail as high as it can sit while keeping the top propeller tip 60 mm under water.

At 125 mm the gimbal's lowest point is level with the keel. Going lower would cost about 90 mm of launching draft for less than 1% more forward thrust.

A correction to what I described before

Last time I put the hinge above the shaft, so that thrust would push the tail down. Drawing it showed that this doesn't work with a straight shaft. A shaft can only bend at a joint that sits on the hinge axis. Move the hinge off the joint and the shaft needs a second joint and a sliding spline. Then, as the tail swings up, the joint angles pass 70°, so the tail would have to unplug from the drive every time it lifted.

Putting the hinge through the joint solves that, but it loses the effect you wanted: thrust no longer holds the tail down. Gravity and a spring do that job instead, and reverse no longer needs a latch.

If you want thrust to hold it down

Thrust can only hold the drive down if the propeller shaft runs below the hinge, parallel to it rather than through it. That takes gears at both ends of a leg, as in an outboard motor's lower unit: a bevel pair at the hinge, a shaft down the leg, and a second bevel pair at the propeller. Ahead thrust then swings the leg forward onto its stop. The cost is two more bevel pairs, a few percent more loss, and a latch for reverse.
